# Freshwater Macroinvertebrate Diversity and Ecology: Technical Brief

Macroinvertebrate assemblages integrate physical, chemical, and biological conditions over weeks to years, making them the workhorse taxon for diagnosing land-use impacts on streams and for evaluating restoration success. The seven items below are the core analytical and conceptual tools practitioners reach for.

1. **Bioassessment via multimetric indices (e.g., IBI, O/E models)**
   Multiple community attributes (taxa richness, %EPT, tolerance-weighted abundance, functional feeding group ratios) are scored against reference-site distributions and combined into a single index of biotic integrity. The causal logic: land-use stressors (nutrients, sediment, thermal alteration) filter sensitive taxa before tolerant ones, so metric response direction and magnitude signal degradation before richness collapses outright. Standard form: an Index of Biotic Integrity (IBI) sums standardized (0–10) metric scores, `IBI = Σ scorei`; the alternative observed/expected (O/E) approach uses predictive models (e.g., RIVPACS) so `O/E = observed taxa / expected taxa given reference conditions`, with O/E ≈ 1 indicating minimal impairment.

2. **Reference condition approach (RCA)**
   Because "pristine" baselines are rare, assessment relies on a population of minimally-disturbed reference sites, stratified by ecoregion or stream type, to define the expected range of natural variability against which test sites are compared. The trade-off is bias-variance: too few/heterogeneous reference sites inflate false impairment calls, while overly narrow stratification loses statistical power. Procedurally this underlies both IBI scoring (metric distributions from reference sites set scoring thresholds/tercile breaks) and O/E models (reference-site environmental predictors calibrate expected taxa lists).

3. **Species sensitivity/tolerance weighting (e.g., Hilsenhoff Biotic Index)**
   Each taxon is assigned an empirical tolerance value reflecting its typical abundance along a pollution (usually organic enrichment/oxygen) gradient; abundance-weighting these values across a sample yields a single stressor-response score. This works because physiological oxygen demand and life-history traits (respiration mode, voltinism) causally constrain which taxa persist under given DO/nutrient regimes. Standard form: `HBI = Σ(ni × tvi) / N`, where ni is the count of taxon i, tvi its tolerance value (0=intolerant, 10=tolerant), and N total individuals.

4. **Functional feeding group (FFG) analysis and the River Continuum Concept**
   Taxa are classified by feeding mechanism (shredders, collectors, scrapers, predators) rather than taxonomy, linking community structure to energy pathways (allochthonous leaf litter vs. autochthonous periphyton). The River Continuum Concept predicts FFG proportions shift systematically from headwaters (shredder/collector-dominated, canopy-shaded) to mid-order rivers (scraper-dominated, algal-based) to large rivers (fine-particle collectors); deviations flag disrupted nutrient/light/organic-matter inputs (e.g., urbanization removing canopy, damming altering particle size). Ratios such as shredder:total or scraper:filterer are compared against continuum expectations rather than fixed thresholds.

5. **Metacommunity theory and dispersal-environment partitioning**
   Local community composition is modeled as jointly determined by environmental filtering (niche-based sorting along stressor gradients) and dispersal processes (connectivity, distance decay, source-sink dynamics), which explains why some degraded sites retain sensitive taxa (rescue effect from nearby refugia) while isolated sites don't recover post-restoration. Variation partitioning decomposes community dissimilarity into environmental, spatial, and shared fractions, typically via redundancy analysis (RDA) or distance-based RDA: `Total variation = [E|S] + [S|E] + [E∩S] + residual`, using Moran's Eigenvector Maps (MEM) or PCNM axes to represent spatial structure.

6. **Multiple-stressor and interaction diagnosis**
   Land-use change rarely acts through a single pathway; nutrient enrichment, sedimentation, thermal shift, flow alteration, and toxicants co-occur and can combine additively, synergistically, or antagonistically. Practitioners use stressor-response curves fit per candidate stressor (often via boosted regression trees or generalized additive models to allow nonlinearity/thresholds) and then test for interaction terms or use structural equation models to separate direct vs. indirect (e.g., nutrients → algae → DO → invertebrates) causal chains, since managing the wrong stressor wastes restoration budget. No single canonical formula; the general regression form is `response ~ stressor1 + stressor2 + stressor1:stressor2 (+ covariates)`, with change-point/threshold analysis (e.g., Threshold Indicator Taxa Analysis, TITAN) used to locate breakpoints.

7. **Flow regime and disturbance ecology (natural flow regime paradigm; resistance/resilience to drought)**
   Magnitude, frequency, duration, timing, and rate-of-change of flow (the "five components") structure macroinvertebrate life-history traits and recolonization dynamics; drought and urbanization-driven flashiness truncate low-flow refugia and increase scour disturbance, selecting for taxa with resistant traits (diapause, small body size, aerial dispersal) over resilient ones. Recovery trajectories after flow disturbance are often modeled with resistance (magnitude of initial decline) and resilience (rate of return to pre-disturbance state) metrics, e.g., resilience as the time constant k in an exponential recovery model `Nt = N∞ − (N∞ − N0)e^(−kt)`, linking hydrologic alteration metrics (e.g., Indicators of Hydrologic Alteration) directly to expected community trait shifts.
