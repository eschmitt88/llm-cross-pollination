# Technical Brief: Taxation and Compliance Studies

This literature studies why taxpayers comply or evade, how enforcement and institutions shape that decision, and how to measure activity that hides from both. The core tension running through the field is that compliance is jointly produced by deterrence (audit probability × penalty) and legitimacy (trust in government, perceived fairness, social norms) — and empirical work spends most of its effort separating the two.

1. **Allingham–Sandmo deterrence model.** The canonical starting point: a rational taxpayer reports income to maximize expected utility, trading off the tax saved by underreporting against the probability of audit times the penalty if caught. It predicts compliance rises with audit probability (p) and penalty rate (f), but empirically over-predicts evasion at real-world audit rates (~1%), motivating the behavioral extensions below.
   *Form:* max over declared income D of E[U] = (1-p)·U(W - θD) + p·U(W - θW - f·θ(W-D)), where W = true income, θ = tax rate.

2. **Slippery Slope Framework.** Reframes compliance as a function of two orthogonal forces — coercive *power* of authorities (audit/penalty capacity) and *trust* in tax authorities/government. High power alone yields "enforced compliance" (fragile, adversarial); high trust yields "voluntary compliance" (stable, cooperative); the framework is used to justify service-oriented over purely punitive enforcement strategy.
   *Form:* Compliance = f(Power, Trust), typically depicted as a 3D surface with a saddle showing compliance is low only when both are low.

3. **Tax morale.** The intrinsic, non-pecuniary willingness to pay taxes even absent deterrence — driven by reciprocity with the state, social norms, and perceived fairness of the fiscal exchange. It's invoked to explain the empirical "compliance puzzle" (why measured evasion is far below what a pure deterrence model predicts) and is measured via survey indices (e.g., World Values Survey items) regressed on institutional-quality covariates.
   *Form:* Compliance_i = β₀ + β₁·Deterrence_i + β₂·TaxMorale_i + β₃·InstitutionalQuality_i + ε_i, with tax morale typically instrumented or proxied by survey Likert scores.

4. **Risk-based audit selection (DIF scoring).** Since universal auditing is infeasible, authorities score returns on a discriminant function of reporting anomalies (deduction ratios, income volatility, sector norms) and audit the highest-scoring subset, concentrating enforcement resources where expected recovered revenue per audit-dollar is highest. This generates selection bias that researchers must correct for (e.g., via random-audit benchmark samples like the US NRP) when estimating true evasion rates.
   *Form:* DIF_i = Σ_k w_k·x_{ik}; audit if DIF_i > threshold, calibrated against a random-audit baseline to estimate detection-corrected tax gap.

5. **Third-party information reporting / withholding.** Compliance is far higher for income subject to third-party reporting (wages, matched via employer W-2/1099-style filings) or automatic withholding than for self-reported income (business/self-employment), because misreporting requires collusion between two independently-verified sources rather than a unilateral lie. This is the single most robust empirical regularity in the compliance literature (Kleven et al. Denmark audit study) and drives policy toward expanding information trails (VAT invoice matching, bank reporting).
   *Form:* Evasion rate ≈ (1 − verification coverage), decomposed empirically as misreporting_self-reported ≫ misreporting_third-party-matched ≈ 0.

6. **Bunching estimator at kinks and notches.** When the tax schedule has a kink (marginal rate jump) or notch (discrete jump in average rate at a threshold), taxpayers with elastic responses cluster their reported income just below the threshold; the excess mass in the empirical income density identifies the elasticity of taxable income without needing an explicit control group. This is the workhorse quasi-experimental technique for estimating both real behavioral response and evasion/avoidance response to tax rates.
   *Form:* e = (ΔB / h) / (Δt / t), where ΔB is excess bunching mass around threshold h and Δt/t is the log change in the net-of-tax rate at the kink.

7. **Shadow economy measurement (MIMIC / currency-demand).** Because informal activity is unobserved by definition, researchers infer its size indirectly: currency-demand approaches regress cash holdings on tax burden and other "causes" after purging a legitimate-transactions baseline, while MIMIC (Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes) models treat shadow-economy size as a latent variable explained by causes (tax burden, regulation, tax morale) and reflected in indicators (cash use, labor-force participation, official GDP growth). Results calibrate cross-country shadow-economy-as-%-GDP series widely used as the dependent variable in compliance-determinant regressions.
   *Form (MIMIC):* η = γ'x + ζ (structural: latent shadow economy driven by causes x); y = λη + ε (measurement: indicators y load on η), estimated via structural equation modeling (LISREL-style).
